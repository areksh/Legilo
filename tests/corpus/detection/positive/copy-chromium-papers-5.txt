Abstract
Annotated reading is generally believed an effective way to improve learners’ reading
competence. However, most existing studies focus on online annotated reading while
neglecting to scaffold students throughout the entire learning process. To address the
gap, this study proposed a four-stage annotated reading framework and employed a
quasi-experimental design to evaluate its effectiveness in developing students’ reading competence. Concurrently, students’ perceptions and actualization of this pedagogy were also investigated. Results demonstrated that this framework had potential
in cultivating students’ reading competence, it was perceived as offering substantially
more benefits than difficulties, and students who yielded greater gains exhibited
higher engagement levels. Furthermore, modes of learning were not the decisive
factor in learning outcomes, and students’ engagement proved equally critical. The
findings can facilitate the field’s re-examination of technology use and shifting more
focus to students’ individual differences and the holistic teaching-learning process.
Targeted pedagogical suggestions were presented based on the findings.
1. Introduction
Reading competence, the ability to read and understand what has been read, is considered a key competence that remains important throughout a person’s life [1], and
ranked among the most pivotal language skills in language education [2]. In English
learning, reading can not only expand learners’ vocabulary [3,4] but also improve
their language output abilities [5], and the transfer of reading skills can be applied to
other language skills such as listening, speaking and writing [6]. Therefore, developing students’ English reading competence is one of the prominent responsibilities for
English teachers.
Annotated reading is believed to be effective in improving learners’ reading
competence [7] and academic performance [8] due to their active engagement with

texts thus resulting in deeper understanding and longer memory retention. With the
development of Internet and modern technology, the medium of reading has shifted
from paper-based reading towards online reading [9], and a variety of digital annotation tools such as Diigo, Hypothesis and Perusall mushroom. It makes social reading,
an online approach to reading that integrates social, interpersonal, and interactive
elements in a collaborative and constructive process [10,11], possible. These tools
provide platforms for readers not only to read and annotate but also to share, discuss or even dispute, and hence foster social interaction. To date, social annotated
reading has been reported to provide contextual, social, and linguistic affordances for
EFL learners [12], reinforce learner engagement [13,14], enhance reading motivation
[15,16] and bring positive affect [16].
However, despite its prosperity, there also exist findings reporting deficiencies of
solely relying on online annotated reading. Paper-based annotated reading demonstrated merits over its online counterpart for it gives readers the opportunity to slow
down and add their own thoughts and comments, raise questions for further investigation, and challenge ideas within the text [17] while relieving the burden of hardware
requirement [18]. Furthermore, both Aydogdu and Winder [19], and Chen and Chen
[20] reported that face-to-face interaction was superior because it fostered better
group dynamics, participation, motivation, and deeper learning compared to online
modes.
To make full use of the pedagogical benefits of paper-based annotated reading, offline group interaction and social reading, we designed a blended four-stage
annotated reading instructional framework. A quasi-experiment with mixed research
methods was conducted in an EFL context to verify its impact on students’ reading
competence as well as explore students’ perceptions of and actualization of their
annotated reading practices. The findings would have implications on annotated
reading instruction and it is also our hope that this instructional design will help inform
future studies.
2. Literature review
2.1. Annotated reading and pedagogical frameworks
Annotated reading, also known as close reading, is a deliberate interaction with a text
with the aim of helping the reader to understand it more fully [17]. Sustained attention can be conceptualized as the core cognitive underpinning for annotated reading.
Öksüz et al. [21] demonstrate that sustained attention serves as a “cognitive bridge”
during reading—it not only directly predicts comprehension levels but also acts as
a full mediator, transforming the efficacy of verbal working memory into accessible reading comprehension ability. Thus, the practice of annotated reading—which
requires readers to continuously select, mark, and reflect on text segments—can be
seen as a natural behavioral expression of sustained attention, training the very process of consistently and strategically allocating attention to key information.
