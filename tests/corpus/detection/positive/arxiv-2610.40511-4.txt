2.1 LANGUAGE ENCODING MODELS
Encoding models test whether a candidate representational space captures information present in
neural responses. In language neuroscience, this approach has progressed from investigator-defined
semantic features to contextual representations learned by neural language models, which predict ac-
tivity across fMRI, MEG, and intracranial recordings (Huth et al., 2016; Jain & Huth, 2018; Toneva
& Wehbe, 2019; Schrimpf et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2022; Caucheteux & King, 2022).
LLM representations are effective encoding spaces because they capture many aspects of language
simultaneously, but this also complicates their interpretation. A single layer may contain thousands
of distributed features reflecting lexical, syntactic, semantic, contextual, and predictive information,
any combination of which may support neural prediction. Thus, successful encoding establishes
that neurally relevant information is linearly accessible from the representation but does not reveal
which components carry that signal.
Prior work has related neural predictivity to properties including next-word prediction performance,
model scale, layer depth, representational generality, intrinsic dimensionality, and particular linguis-
tic or computational features (Schrimpf et al., 2021; Antonello & Huth, 2024; Antonello & Cheng,
2024; Kumar et al., 2024; Mischler et al., 2024). These results constrain possible explanations of
LLM–brain alignment but primarily characterize representations globally rather than identifying the
specific directions responsible for prediction.
2.2 BRAIN-PREDICTIVE SUBSPACES
Prior work suggests that both language representations and their relationship to neural activity ex-
hibit low-dimensional structure. Antonello et al. (2021) identified low-dimensional organization
in linguistic representations related to fMRI predictivity, while Lewis et al. (2025) used low-rank
constraints to factorize LLM-to-MEG encoding models into interpretable semantic, temporal, and
spatial components.
These approaches motivate a more direct question: how much of an individual LLM representation
is actually required to recover its neural predictivity? Reduced-rank regression provides a super-
vised way to answer this question by identifying low-dimensional directions chosen specifically for
their ability to predict neural responses. This distinguishes the brain-predictive subspace from un-
supervised low-dimensional structure, such as the principal variance directions identified by PCA,
and provides a representation whose dimensionality, generalization, and linguistic content can be
studied directly.
